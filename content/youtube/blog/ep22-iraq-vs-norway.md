## The secret nobody talks about

Jakarta, the 29th of July, 2007. Back home, Iraq was at war — bombs, checkpoints, a nation split between Sunni, Shia and Kurd. But the team was all three, Sunni, Shia and Kurd in one dressing room, playing for one flag. Their coach, Jorvan Vieira, a Brazilian, had been with them only two months, and nobody gave them a chance. Then came the final against Saudi Arabia. In the 72nd minute a corner swung in, captain Younis Mahmoud rose above everyone, and headed it home. 1-0. Iraq were champions of Asia — a war-torn nation, the best team on the continent, the greatest day Iraqi football has ever known. And yet that joy carried a wound: bombs struck the crowds who poured into the streets to celebrate. They danced anyway. They had to.

## The Lions of Mesopotamia

That is what this shirt carries, and tonight the grandsons of those players try again. Iraq, the Lions of Mesopotamia, are built on heart, on history, and on a refusal to be written off. In midfield, Zidane Iqbal — yes, named after Zinedine Zidane — once at Manchester United, now the brain of the team. Up front, Aymen Hussein and Ali Al-Hamadi, strikers who fight for every ball like the shirt demands, with Amir Al-Ammari driving from deep and Rebin Sulaka holding the wall. Underdogs, and proud of it.

## The storm from the north

But across the line stands a wave of red and a force of nature. Norway are back at a World Cup for the first time since 1998 — twenty-eight years away from the biggest stage. At the spear, Erling Haaland, their all-time top scorer at twenty-five, with sixteen goals in qualifying alone — a machine built to score. Beside him, captain Martin Odegaard, all silk and vision, the one who sets the machine running. With Sorloth, Nusa and Berge, this is a golden generation, and they beat Italy home and away to get here. So here is the duel: Iraqi heart against Norwegian power, the survivors against the storm.
